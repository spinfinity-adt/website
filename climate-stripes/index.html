Climate Stripes: A Data Performance was a project I did as part of a performance talk at VizChitra 2025, India's first DataViz Conference. A lot of fun mini artifacts led to the creation of this piece, so I made this page to house them all!

The dataset used here is the climate stripes: Ed Hawkins' visualization from the University of Reading — 165 years of global average temperature anomalies rendered as colored stripes, blue to red. People have adopted it into all kinds of forms: pottery, glass, it's on Greta Thunberg's book cover. I used that dataset.

My outputs were a data performance and an audience interaction where we experienced the warming stripes at our fingertips (literally!).

01 — The Performance

Climate Stripes: a data performance

I broke the full 165 years of warming stripes into decades, and in the live performance, each decade played for about thirty seconds. The video couldn't capture the long exposure trails created, so I recreated it in TouchDesigner — this time, with each year getting a second, and a data overlay showing the year and temperature. You can check out the long exposure photographs below to get a sense of the stripes. I used bits of the Laban Movement framework to choreograph this - the blues were slow and light, and as we moved to the reds, it became fast, strong and sustained.

01

The prototype

Initially I wanted to program one color per second into the poi. That was way too much work. My partner Jagrut, who works at the Science Gallery Bengaluru, had a simpler idea: just upload segments of the stripes as images instead of changing colors every single second. At least for a prototype, that would work. We found a dark room at the gallery, he took some long-exposure photos, and we had a 30-second demo ready.

02

The data

Getting the actual hex codes wasn't super simple, to be honest. I found a blog by someone crocheting a climate stripes blanket — they'd mapped out all the colors as hex codes. I fed those into Pillow (Python) to generate the poi images. In between the color segments, I kept inserting the year as text, so the audience could track where in time we were.

03

The music

After a couple of tries, Hans Zimmer's Time turned out to be perfect. It has these natural 30-second segments and a crescendo that builds and builds. Two and a half minutes of climate history, synced to the music. For the proposal teaser, I'd used Tool's Lateralus — I liked how the music progressed, mirroring how the stripes progress. But Time was the one for the full performance.

04

The choreography

Using the Laban movement framework from Shabari's workshop, I mapped temperature values to physical expression. As the years progress and the anomaly rises, the spinning moves from slow and light to fast, heavy, and sudden.

05

The ending

This was the hardest part. Jagrut suggested reversing the slides — ending on a note of hope, showing the blues returning. But I didn't want false hope. I thought about uploading a burning earth image to the poi, but that would've been tacky. And I didn't have enough time to prototype it anyway — I also had to put together the talk itself, and the whole thing came together in about a month and a half. What we finally landed on: 2030? A question mark. Different colors spinning all at once. What will the next stripe be? That's up to us.

02 — Audience Interaction

Surprise Community Data Sonification!

300 of us heated up the room with our palms — in an attempt to experience the rise in heat the earth has gone through in the last 160 years.

I panned the stripes on the screen, and as it went from dark blue to dark red, I asked participants to rub their hands faster. We generated heat in our palms and data sonification also happened, exactly as I'd hoped! The AC was also particularly effective in the room, so folks enjoyed this exercise a little extra!

03 — The Concept

What is data visceralization?

I first came across this term in Data Feminism (D'Ignazio & Klein), in the chapter "Elevate Emotion and Embodiment." A good pun gets etched in my brain. Whenever I find an intriguing term, I like to look at how the word came to be — the same way a data nerd looks at the source of their data, I look at the source of the word.

visceral
adjective · vis·cer·al
"affecting inward feelings"
from viscera — Latin for internal organs
← Medieval Latin visceralis, "internal"
← plural of viscus, meaning flesh, innermost part

The bowels were regarded as the seat of emotion.
data
noun · da·ta
"a fact given or granted"
from datum — Latin for "thing given"
← neuter past participle of dare, "to give"
← a fact given as the basis for calculation

Challenge the givens, and you can change the conversation.
Raw data doesn't exist. It's always processed — sometimes by our sensors, often by our guts.

Data visceralization is making data felt in the body, not just seen with the eyes. Visual things are for the eyes. Visceralizations are for the whole body — data you see, hear, feel, breathe. Term coined by Kelly Dobson at RISD (2010), via Data Feminism.
